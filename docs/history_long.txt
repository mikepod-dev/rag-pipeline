The history of coffee cultivation dates back centuries. According to legend, coffee was first discovered in Ethiopia by a goat herder named Kaldi, who noticed his goats became energetic after eating berries from a certain tree. Word of the energizing berries spread, and coffee cultivation eventually reached the Arabian Peninsula by the 15th century. Coffee houses began appearing in cities across the Middle East, becoming popular social gathering places. By the 17th century, coffee had spread to Europe, where it was initially met with suspicion by some religious leaders before becoming widely accepted. The Dutch were among the first Europeans to successfully cultivate coffee outside of Arabia, establishing plantations in Java. Coffee then made its way to the Americas in the 18th century, brought by a French naval officer who transported a single coffee plant across the Atlantic Ocean. That single plant is believed to be the ancestor of millions of coffee trees now grown throughout Central and South America. Brazil eventually became the world's largest coffee producer, a position it still holds today. The specific year the French officer completed this journey was 1723, and the plant survived a difficult voyage that included a storm, a suspected saboteur, and a severe drought at sea.
